The Hisense U7 is the set that made a very large, very good television affordable in Middle Tennessee. The 75-inch version — model code **75U7SG** — turns up in family rooms that were not really built with a screen that size in mind, which is most family rooms.

It also has one quirk that generates more questions than any other TV we mount. We will get to it.

---

## Step 1: The Wall, Before Anything Else

A 75-inch panel typically runs somewhere in the 60 to 85 lb range. The wall does not care that the TV was a bargain, and Hisense buyers are the group most likely to have been told that a good drywall anchor will do.

It will not. **The bracket has to be lagged into the studs.**

Find them with a stud finder, mark every hit, then confirm each mark with a second pass from the other direction before you trust it. Aim to centre the bracket across two studs; on standard 16-inch framing a 75-inch mount will often reach three, and you should take three whenever the spacing offers it.

If the wall turns out to be masonry, plaster over lath, or metal-stud partition — all of which exist in Nashville homes — it will still hold the TV, but the hardware changes. Our surcharge for concrete, tile, stone or metal starts at **$25**; drywall carries no extra charge.

## Step 2: Now, About the Depth

Here is the quirk.

The U7 uses **Mini-LED backlighting**, and a Mini-LED backlight needs physical room behind the panel. The chassis is meaningfully deeper than the thin edge-lit LED television it is probably replacing.

This surprises people. We get asked about it on Hisense more than any other brand — some version of *"why does it stick out so much more than my old one?"* — usually after the TV is already on the wall.

It is not a fault, it is not a bad install, and no other installer would make it go away. It is the backlight. What you can do is choose the bracket around it:

- A **slim fixed mount** keeps the U7 as close to the wall as the chassis allows. This is the right answer if a flat, built-in look is what you are after.
- A **tilt mount** adds a little depth and buys you a few degrees of downward angle, which is useful if the TV sits higher than ideal.
- A **full-motion arm** adds the most depth of all, in exchange for being able to swing the screen towards a kitchen or around a corner.

You cannot have flush and full-motion. Decide which you actually want **before** the holes are drilled, because it is a bracket decision and it is much cheaper to make once.

---

## Step 3: Height, From Where You Sit

Centre of the screen at seated eye level — roughly 42 to 48 inches off the floor for a standard sofa.

At 75 inches, check that against the actual room rather than the tape measure. The screen is tall enough that a textbook centre height can drop the bottom edge to where a soundbar or a console starts getting in the way. Put the furniture where it will live, sit down, have someone hold a mark on the wall, and look at it before you commit.

---

## Step 4: Two People, Non-Negotiable

A 75-inch U7 is wider than one person's arm span. You cannot hold the weight and feel for the bracket hooks at the same time — and that exact moment is when panels crack and walls get gouged.

One person takes the weight, the other guides the TV onto the rail and confirms it has seated. We send two technicians on every 75-inch install. If you are doing this yourself, recruit someone before you start rather than halfway through.

---

## Step 5: Cables and a Real Test

Plan the cable route before the bracket goes on the wall, because in-wall routing depends on exactly where the television ends up.

There is a small silver lining to the Mini-LED depth here: a deeper chassis leaves slightly more working room behind the panel than a razor-thin set does, which occasionally makes the in-wall route easier rather than harder. Concealment starts **from $60 per TV**; a paintable surface raceway is the fallback where the wall cannot be opened.

Then test properly. Power up, cycle every input, confirm the soundbar and any console actually pass through the set. Do not assume.
